Birds of Great Britain · a closer look

What Changed, and Why

Every plate in this gallery whose classification has moved since 1873, gathered together and grouped by the kind of change — genus reassignment, epithet correction, DNA-driven revision, or full lumping into another species. 213 stories in total. The facts here are the same ones already on each species' own card in the main gallery; this page just groups them a different way.

Confirmed or overturned by DNA evidence (11)

Cases where molecular/genomic studies -- not just re-examining anatomy -- were what actually settled the question.

Alpine Swift Tachymarptis melba

Split out of Cypselus/Apus into its own genus Tachymarptis on the basis of size and structure, later confirmed by DNA phylogenetics.

Coal Tit Periparus ater

Split out of Parus into Periparus, based on DNA phylogenetics.

Eurasian Blue Tit Cyanistes caeruleus
Gould’s plate: “Blue Tit”

Split out of Parus into Cyanistes along with the other 'blue tit' relatives, based on DNA phylogenetics.

European Crested Tit Lophophanes cristatus
Gould’s plate: “Crested Tit”

Split out of Parus into Lophophanes, based on DNA phylogenetics.

Greater Spotted Eagle Clanga clanga
Gould’s plate: “Spotted Eagle”

Genus revision split the spotted eagles out of Aquila into Clanga, based on DNA phylogenetics.

Lesser Spotted Woodpecker Dryobates minor

Split out from Dendrocopos into its own genus, Dryobates, based on DNA phylogenetics -- a further split than its larger spotted relatives above underwent.

Northern Goshawk Accipiter gentilis
Gould’s plate: “Goshawk”

Genus revision folded Astur into Accipiter for most of the 20th and early 21st centuries; some recent DNA-based classifications have proposed reviving Astur for this species, so the genus placement is not entirely settled.

Ruff Calidris pugnax

Moved from Machetes (and later, for much of the 20th century, its own genus Philomachus) into Calidris, following DNA evidence that the sandpipers form one closely related group with no clean dividing lines between the old genera. This plate shows the adult male and female (Reeve); plate 249 (same account) shows the plumage of the first autumn.

Sanderling Calidris alba

Genus unchanged -- Gould's own heading already used Calidris, a genus he applied narrowly to just this one bird, long before molecular work in the 2000s showed that Calidris should be expanded to absorb Tringa canutus, Machetes pugnax, and most of the other small sandpipers in this volume (see the Knot, Ruff, and stints above). The species epithet itself was standardised to alba, replacing arenaria.

Siberian Thrush Geokichla sibirica

Moved from Gould's genus Cichloselys into Geokichla, based on DNA phylogenetics.

Snowy Owl Bubo scandiacus

Genus revision has moved this species repeatedly — from Nyctea to its own genus, and more recently DNA evidence folded it into Bubo alongside the eagle-owls.

Split off into its own new genus (6)

A genus that used to hold this species (often alongside close relatives) was divided, and this species ended up in a newly recognised genus of its own.

Black Grouse Lyrurus tetrix
Gould’s plate: “Blackcock”

Split from Tetrao (retained for the Capercaillie) into its own genus Lyrurus, reflecting the lyre-shaped tail Gould's own text singles out as diagnostic.

European Shag Gulosus aristotelis
Gould’s plate: “Crested Cormorant, or Shag”

Split off from Phalacrocorax into its own genus, Gulosus, with the epithet changed to aristotelis.

European Stonechat Saxicola rubicola
Gould’s plate: “Stone-chat or Furze-chat”

Moved from Pratincola into Saxicola; the European Stonechat was also later split from the Siberian and African Stonechats, which now form separate species.

Little Bustard Tetrax tetrax

Split off from Otis (kept for the Great Bustard) into its own genus, Tetrax -- a case of the species epithet becoming the genus name.

Northern Wheatear Oenanthe oenanthe
Gould’s plate: “Wheatear”

Split out of Saxicola into its own genus Oenanthe (the wheatears), separate from the chats.

Woodlark Lullula arborea
Gould’s plate: “Wood-Lark”

Split out of Alauda into its own genus, Lullula, based on structural and vocal differences from the true larks.

An old genus was folded back into a larger one (3)

The reverse of a split: a genus Gould used (sometimes his own coinage) turned out not to be distinct enough to keep, and was absorbed into a bigger, older genus.

Common Kestrel Falco tinnunculus
Gould’s plate: “Kestrel”

Genus revision folded Tinnunculus back into Falco.

Red-footed Falcon Falco vespertinus
Gould’s plate: “Orange-legged Hobby”

Genus revision folded Erythropus back into Falco.

Rough-legged Buzzard Buteo lagopus

Gould placed this species in its own genus, Archibuteo; that genus was later folded back into Buteo.

Moved to a different genus (118)

The most common story by far: same species, same species epithet in most cases, just reassigned to a different genus as relationships were reassessed.

Species epithet standardised or corrected (52)

The genus was already right (sometimes even in Gould's own printed heading) -- only the second half of the two-part name changed, usually because an earlier-published name was found and took priority under nomenclature rules.

Arctic Tern Sterna paradisaea

Epithet standardised to paradisaea, replacing Gould's macrura -- see the note on the preceding Roseate Tern plate for how that name had, in Gould's own day, been applied instead to that other species.

Folded into another species entirely (7)

Gould treated these as their own kind; modern taxonomy folded them into another species as a subspecies or colour form instead.

Black-bellied Dipper Cinclus cinclus
Gould’s plate: “Black-bellied Water-Ouzel”

No longer treated as a distinct species; it is now considered a Scandinavian/continental subspecies of the White-throated Dipper, Cinclus cinclus melanogaster.

Bluethroat Luscinia svecica
Gould’s plate: “White-throated Bluebreast”

No longer treated as a separate species from the Red-throated Bluebreast; the white-spotted form is now considered the subspecies Luscinia svecica cyanecula of a single Bluethroat species.

Red Grouse Lagopus lagopus scotica

No longer treated as its own species -- folded into Lagopus lagopus (Willow Ptarmigan/Willow Grouse) as the endemic British and Irish subspecies scotica, distinguished mainly by never turning white in winter.

Western Yellow Wagtail Motacilla flava
Gould’s plate: “Grey-headed Wagtail”

Moved from Budytes into Motacilla; this and the next two plates (Budytes rayi, flava, cinereocapilla) are all now treated as subspecies within one wide-ranging, highly variable species, the Western Yellow Wagtail -- a striking case of Gould's three 'species' collapsing into subspecies of one.

Western Yellow Wagtail (Ashy-headed subspecies) Motacilla flava cinereocapilla
Gould’s plate: “Grey-capped Wagtail”

Moved from Budytes into Motacilla; treated today as the Ashy-headed subspecies of the Western Yellow Wagtail, Motacilla flava cinereocapilla, not a separate species -- see the note on the Grey-headed Wagtail two plates above.

Western Yellow Wagtail (British subspecies) Motacilla flava flavissima
Gould’s plate: “Yellow Wagtail”

The genus Budytes was absorbed into Motacilla, and this British breeding form -- named for the naturalist John Ray -- is now treated as the subspecies Motacilla flava flavissima rather than its own species.

White Wagtail (Pied Wagtail) Motacilla alba yarrellii
Gould’s plate: “Pied Wagtail”

Gould treated this as its own species, distinct from the Continental White Wagtail two plates over. It is now treated as a British/Irish subspecies of the White Wagtail, Motacilla alba yarrellii, not a full species in its own right.

Other / mixed stories (16)

Reclassifications that do not reduce to a single clean pattern -- often more than one thing changed at once.

Barn Owl Tyto alba
Gould’s plate: “Barn-Owl”

Genus revision separated the barn owls from the typical owls, moving this species from Strix to Tyto.

Common Grasshopper Warbler Locustella naevia
Gould’s plate: “Grasshopper Warbler”

Gould's own heading already places this bird correctly in Locustella; only the species epithet has changed, from Ray's old avicula to the currently accepted naevia (Boddaert).

Common Nightingale Luscinia megarhynchos
Gould’s plate: “Nightingale”

Gould's own heading already places this bird in the modern genus, Luscinia -- but under the epithet philomela, which today denotes a different, closely related species (the Thrush Nightingale, a scarce vagrant to Britain). The bird breeding in England that Gould describes here is the Common Nightingale, whose accepted binomial is Luscinia megarhynchos.

Common Swift Apus apus
Gould’s plate: “Swift”

The old genus Cypselus was absorbed into Apus, the currently recognised swift genus.

Common Whitethroat Curruca communis
Gould’s plate: “Whitethroat”

A large recent revision (2020s) of the Old World warblers split most Sylvia species into a restored genus Curruca; the Whitethroat's accepted binomial is now Curruca communis. Gould's own heading already uses Sylvia (Sylvia cinerea); the older Linnaean synonym, Motacilla sylvia, printed in his synonymy line, reflects a placement that was outdated even in his own day.

Eurasian Wren Troglodytes troglodytes
Gould’s plate: “Common Wren”

Gould's own heading already prints the modern genus, 'TROGLODYTES EUROPAUS'; the synonymy's older 'Passer troglodytes, Will.' reflects an even earlier, pre-Linnaean-tradition placement (John Willughby's 17th-century Ornithology). The accepted binomial today is Troglodytes troglodytes -- only the species epithet has changed.

Garden Warbler Sylvia borin
Gould’s plate: “Garden-Warbler”

Gould's own heading places this bird in Curruca (Curruca hortensis) -- but the Garden Warbler stayed in Sylvia during the 2020s genus revision, becoming Sylvia borin; the species epithet also changed entirely, from hortensis to borin.

Great Reed Warbler Acrocephalus arundinaceus
Gould’s plate: “Thrush Warbler”

Gould's own heading already places this bird in Acrocephalus; only the species epithet has changed, from turdoides to arundinaceus. The modern English name 'Great Reed Warbler' has replaced Gould's 'Thrush Warbler'.

Gyrfalcon Falco rusticolus
Gould’s plate: “Greenland Falcon”

Now treated as a colour morph of the single, wide-ranging Gyrfalcon, Falco rusticolus, rather than a distinct species. This plate's account (pp. 209-210) also covers the Iceland Falcon plate (p. 205) and the dark-race plate (p. 215).

Gyrfalcon Falco rusticolus
Gould’s plate: “Norwegian or Gyrfalcon”

Along with the Iceland and Greenland Falcons, this form is now treated as part of one variable species, Falco rusticolus; Gould's gyrfalco is a synonym rather than a separate taxon.

Long-eared Owl Asio otus

Genus revision moved this species from Otus to Asio.

Red-backed Shrike Lanius collurio
Gould’s plate: “Butcher Bird”

The genus Enneoctonus was absorbed back into Lanius; the modern English name is 'Red-backed Shrike' rather than the old folk name 'Butcher Bird'.

Ring Ouzel Turdus torquatus
Gould’s plate: “Ring-Ousel”

Gould's own heading uses the genus Merula (Merula torquata); modern taxonomy reverted to the original Linnaean placement, Turdus torquatus -- which the synonymy line on this same page already lists as the older alternative, 'Turdus torquatus, Linn.', and that turned out to be the name that stuck.

Short-eared Owl Asio flammeus

Genus revision moved this species from Brachyotus to Asio, alongside the Long-eared Owl.

Song Thrush Turdus philomelos
Gould’s plate: “Thrush”

The name Turdus musicus was dropped for this species (the epithet had also been mis-applied to the Redwing by other authors, causing real confusion); the accepted binomial is now Turdus philomelos.

Tawny Owl Strix aluco
Gould’s plate: “Tawny or Brown Owl”

Genus revision moved this species from Syrnium back into Strix.
