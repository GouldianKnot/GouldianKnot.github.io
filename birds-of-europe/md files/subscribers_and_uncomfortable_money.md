# The View from the Subscription List: Access, Institutions, and Uncomfortable Money

*A research note prompted by Jan's own find: photographs of the "List of Subscribers" printed at the front of Gould's* The Birds of Great Britain *(1862–73), cross-checked here against a clean OCR text layer in the source PDF (Volume I). This is a genuinely useful primary source, and it answers several open questions from earlier in this project more precisely than guesswork could.*

## An important caveat before anything else

This subscriber list belongs to *The Birds of Great Britain* (1862–73) — a later, separate Gould publication, not *The Birds of Europe* (1832–37), which is this gallery's main subject. Gould's subscriber base likely shifted over thirty years of growing fame, so this list tells us about his position at the height of his career, in his last decade, rather than about the specific patrons of the earlier Birds of Europe volumes this gallery presents. It is still directly relevant — it's the same publishing model, the same man, and the only actual subscriber list available to check — but the two shouldn't be silently conflated.

## Did the Birmingham and Midland Institute subscribe?

No — not under that name, at least. A full check of the list (all ~600 entries, cross-referenced against the PDF's OCR text) turns up no "Birmingham and Midland Institute." What it does turn up is **"Birmingham Public Library, The"** as a direct institutional subscriber, alongside two individual Birmingham residents (H. Buckley of Edgbaston and J. Jones and T. Scott, both listed simply as "Birmingham"). So the honest answer to the original question is: the BMI itself doesn't appear to have subscribed, but Birmingham's public library did — meaning Gould's actual, physical volumes would have been accessible to the city's residents through that library rather than (as far as this evidence shows) through the BMI specifically.

## Real evidence of wider dissemination beyond wealthy private buyers

This is the most useful thing the list settles: it is not simply page after page of individual aristocrats. A genuine cross-section of public and institutional subscribers appears throughout, meaning ordinary readers without the means to buy an expensive folio outright could plausibly have seen Gould's actual plates through these institutions:

- **Public and subscription libraries:** Birmingham Public Library, Liverpool Library, Liverpool Free Library, Hull Subscription Library, Plymouth Public Library, the Public Library of Malta.
- **University and college libraries:** Cambridge University, Glasgow University, Edinburgh University, St Andrews University, King's College Cambridge, Downing College Cambridge, Trinity College Dublin, the Bodleian Library and Radcliffe Library (Oxford), Sion College (London).
- **National and royal institutions:** The British Museum, the Royal Institution of Great Britain, the Royal Artillery Institution, the Royal College of Surgeons of England, the Royal Society of Edinburgh, the Royal Library of Brussels, the Royal Library of Berlin.
- **Overseas and colonial institutions:** the Library of the Parliament of Canada, the Imperial Academy of Sciences of St Petersburg, the Philadelphia Academy of Sciences, the Peabody Institute (Baltimore), the Buffalo Natural History Society, the National Museum of Melbourne and the Public Library, Museum and National Gallery of Melbourne, the Zoological Society of London.

This is genuine, primary-sourced evidence — not inference — that Gould's own lifetime publishing model already reached well beyond a closed circle of private wealth, into public libraries and learned institutions whose readers would not themselves have needed to be rich. It doesn't erase the fact that the *original* purchase was an elite transaction; it does show the work didn't stay sealed inside that elite once purchased.

## The uncomfortable money question — what this list actually shows

Jan's original question was whether Gould's subscribers could be shown to include people who profited from the slave trade. This list can't answer that comprehensively (there are roughly 600 names, most identifiable only by title and country estate, and checking each one against a proper slavery-compensation database would be a genuine archival project). But it does hand us one specific, well-documented case rather than speculation.

Three members of the **Rothschild family** appear as individual private subscribers: Sir Anthony de Rothschild, Baronet; Miss Alice de Rothschild (of Aston Clinton, Tring); and "The Baroness" Rothschild (107 Piccadilly). Separately and independently, N M Rothschild & Sons — the same family's banking house — is a documented part of the story of British slavery's *aftermath*: in 1835, a consortium including Nathan Mayer Rothschild and Moses Montefiore won the contract to raise the £20 million the British government needed to pay compensation to former slave-owners after abolition (the compensation, it's worth remembering, went to the owners, not the enslaved). This is confirmed directly by the Rothschild Archive's own record of "the West India Loan."

It is important to be precise about what this does and doesn't show. Being the government's financial contractor for a compensation loan is not the same as having owned slaves or profited directly from the slave trade itself — the Rothschilds' documented role here is as bankers financing a state transaction after the fact, not as plantation owners or slave traders. It is a real, uncomfortable thread — British high finance profiting from arranging the very mechanism that paid slavery's owners rather than its victims — but it should be stated as exactly that, not stretched into something it isn't.

Beyond the Rothschilds, the list is thick with landed aristocratic titles whose family fortunes have well-documented and mixed origins — dukes of Northumberland, Devonshire, Sutherland, Westminster, Wellington, Portland, and Buccleuch and Queensberry all appear, among many others. Some of these families' wealth has been traced to compensation payments or colonial plantation income by historians using UCL's "Legacies of British Slave-ownership" database (a free, publicly searchable record of the roughly 46,000 people compensated in 1833–35); others' fortunes derive from entirely different sources (land, industry, earlier feudal grants). Checking each name against that database individually would be the honest way to go further, and it is a real, doable project — just not one this note completes. What can be said now is only what's shown above: one specific, documented, and carefully-qualified case, not a verdict on the subscriber list as a whole.

## Claims flagged as unverified or approximate

- This note covers only the subscriber list to *The Birds of Great Britain* (1862–73), not *The Birds of Europe* (1832–37); the two lists are very likely different in membership and should not be treated as interchangeable.
- The identification of "Dickinson, C. S., Esq." and similar entries with any specific well-known historical figure has not been attempted here and should not be assumed.
- No systematic check of the aristocratic subscribers against the UCL Legacies of British Slave-ownership database has been carried out; the note names the Rothschild case specifically because it could be confirmed directly, not because it is necessarily the most significant connection on the list.
- The OCR transcription of the subscriber list (via the source PDF) contains minor recognition errors (e.g. "Hsq." for "Esq.") typical of automated text extraction from a 19th-century typeface; names have been cross-checked against Jan's own screenshots where both were available, but errors may remain uncorrected in entries not double-checked this way.
