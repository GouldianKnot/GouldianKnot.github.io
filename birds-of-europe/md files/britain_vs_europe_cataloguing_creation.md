# The Wider Story: Cataloguing "Creation" Across Britain, Europe, and the Atlantic World

*A comparative research note prompted by a fair observation: that the Gould gallery's "Where They Worked" map looks very Eurocentric — and, once that was traced through to an American parallel (Audubon, Peale, the Wilkes Expedition — see this project's companion note on the American story), a much bigger question followed. Was the 18th–19th century drive to catalogue "the whole of Creation" a peculiarly British or English-speaking preoccupation, riding on the coat-tails of empire — or was it happening just as intensively across the courts, universities, and learned societies of continental Europe, for reasons that had little to do with the British Empire at all?*

*This note is the product of a dedicated research pass across Britain, France, the German lands and Austria-Hungary, Italy, Russia, the Netherlands, and Scandinavia. It deliberately keeps this comparative material separate from the Gould-specific reports, since it has grown well beyond a footnote to that project.*

## The short answer

No, it was not a peculiarly Anglophone obsession — but the *shape* the obsession took differed sharply from country to country, and those differences map remarkably well onto each country's underlying political structure. Britain's version really was closely bound up with private wealth, learned societies, and (increasingly, as the 19th century went on) the logistics of empire. France's version was a matter of explicit state policy from the very first year of the Revolution. The German lands, fragmented until 1871, produced something closer to a decentralized network of universities and civic societies. Austria-Hungary looked more like France — a single dynastic-turned-state museum — but built on Habsburg court tradition rather than revolutionary ideology. Russia ran an imperial-scientific-academy model funded directly by the Tsars. The Dutch version was frankly commercial, run through the East India Company's colonial administration. And Sweden's Linnaeus supplied something all of these projects depended on regardless of their politics: the actual grammar — binomial nomenclature — that let anyone's catalogue talk to anyone else's.

## Britain: private wealth and learned societies, increasingly riding on imperial infrastructure

The clearest finding from this research is that British natural history cataloguing in this period was **not primarily a top-down instrument of state empire policy**. It began, and for a long stretch of the 18th and 19th centuries continued, as the enthusiasm of private individuals and self-governing societies: the Royal Society (founded 1660, a private fellowship that received a royal charter for prestige but little sustained treasury money); the Linnean Society (1788), founded when the botanist James Edward Smith personally bought Linnaeus's own collection; the Zoological Society of London (1826), founded by Sir Stamford Raffles and funded by Fellows' subscriptions and (from 1847) paying visitors to its zoo. John Gould's own operation — the subject of the rest of this gallery — is the textbook case: a private, subscription-funded publishing business, not a government department.

Where the state does show up clearly is in the logistics-heavy end of things: the Royal Navy's survey voyages (Cook's *Endeavour*, Darwin's *Beagle*, the *Challenger* expedition of 1872–76 that essentially founded oceanography), and the Geological Survey of Great Britain (1835), which was — tellingly — administered by the *military* Board of Ordnance rather than a civilian scientific ministry. But even here the pattern is usually hybrid rather than purely state-driven: Joseph Banks paid roughly £10,000 of his own money to bring his own retinue aboard the Navy-owned *Endeavour*; Darwin's family paid his expenses aboard the Navy-owned *Beagle*; the *Challenger*'s scientific mission was proposed and staffed by the Royal Society, a private body, which then borrowed a ship from the Navy. State infrastructure carried privately arranged science, again and again.

The clearest sign of a *shift* toward state ownership comes from the empire's administrative machinery specifically. The East India Company's museum (founded 1798) and the Asiatic Society of Bengal (1784, founded by company servants) both began as private or quasi-private enthusiasm among company employees, and were only gradually absorbed into formal state funding and control — the future Indian Museum in Kolkata started drawing on public salaries from 1836 and wasn't formalised by its own Museum Act until 1866, with full state absorption only following the Company's dissolution after 1858. Kew Gardens followed the same arc: run informally by Joseph Banks through personal patronage networks from the 1770s, then formally transferred from Crown to state control in 1840. By contrast, the Australian Museum in Sydney (1827) was state-initiated from day one, funded by a direct Colonial Office grant — showing that once the administrative habit of running colonies was well established, a newer colonial museum could simply be built as a state institution from the start, without needing to pass through an earlier private phase.

So the honest characterisation is: British "cataloguing Creation" began as private and voluntary, and became progressively state-institutionalised over the 19th century, with the empire's shipping, military, and administrative infrastructure supplying the connective tissue that let private enthusiasts operate on a global scale.

## France: an explicit instrument of the state, through three regimes

France offers the sharpest possible contrast, and it is a genuinely well-documented one rather than an inference. The Muséum national d'Histoire naturelle in Paris was created by decree of the National Convention on 10 June 1793 — in the middle of the Revolution — with **twelve professorships of equal rank, all salaried directly by the state**: Lamarck's chair (invertebrates) paid him roughly 2,500 francs a year from the treasury; Cuvier's chair (comparative anatomy) came with a state salary and, over his career, a string of direct government posts — a seat on Napoleon's Council of the Imperial University, chairmanships of educational commissions in annexed territories, and eventually the Council of State itself. This was not a private learned society that happened to receive a royal charter; it was, from its first year, a ministry-style state institution, and it stayed that way across the Revolution, Napoleon's Empire, and the Restoration monarchy that followed — the regime changed three times in thirty years, and the funding mechanism never did.

The Muséum's institutional predecessor, the Jardin du Roi, was itself explicitly royal: Buffon held his post as its Intendant by direct royal appointment from 1739, and used it to compile his monumental *Histoire Naturelle* (1749–1804) as, in effect, a state project. And the pattern continues into France's own exploring expeditions: the Baudin expedition to Australia (1800–1804) was formally approved by Napoleon as First Consul and returned over 200,000 specimens directly to the Muséum and the Jardin des Plantes; the *Description de l'Égypte* (1809–1828), produced by more than 150 state-organised scholars following Napoleon's Egyptian campaign, is arguably the most explicit "cataloguing the whole of Creation [and civilisation]" project of the entire era, and it was a direct arm of Napoleonic state policy from the outset.

## The German lands and Austria-Hungary: two very different answers to the same problem of fragmentation

Germany did not exist as a unified state until 1871, and its natural history cataloguing shows exactly what you'd expect from that fact: no single national apparatus, but a dense patchwork of **university museums funded by individual territorial states** (Göttingen under Hanover, where Johann Friedrich Blumenbach held his chair from 1776; Berlin's collections, funded by Prussia but administered through the university from 1810) and **independent civic or scholarly societies** that filled the gap a unified nation-state would otherwise have filled: the Leopoldina academy (founded 1652 by four private physicians, now Germany's national academy of sciences but originally nothing of the sort), the Gesellschaft Naturforschender Freunde zu Berlin (1773, a private dozen-person collectors' club that grew into a real institution), and the Senckenberg Nature Research Society in Frankfurt (1817, a civic foundation prompted by Goethe). Only after 1871 does anything resembling coordination appear (the German Zoological Society, 1890), and even then the museum landscape stayed spread across former princely capitals — Berlin, Dresden, Stuttgart, Munich, Hamburg, Frankfurt — rather than concentrating in one city the way Paris or Vienna did.

Austria-Hungary is the interesting foil here, because it was also a multi-ethnic, sprawling polity, yet its natural history cataloguing was genuinely centralised — because it inherited something Germany's fragmented princely states never had: a single continuous Habsburg imperial court. The line runs directly from Emperor Franz I's 1748 purchase of a private mineral cabinet, through Maria Theresa's 1776 decision to have it professionally systematised, through the Novara Expedition (1857–59, the first Austrian circumnavigation, explicitly a state Imperial Navy project bringing back some 26,000 specimens), to the opening of the k.k. Naturhistorisches Hofmuseum in Vienna in 1889 — with the Novara Expedition's own chief geologist, Ferdinand von Hochstetter, becoming the new museum's first superintendent. One dynasty, one collection, one museum: the closest thing to the French model outside France, but built on court tradition rather than revolutionary ideology.

## Italy, Russia, the Netherlands, and Sweden: three more variations on the same theme

Italy, like Germany, was politically fragmented until 1861, and its natural history institutions show the same piecemeal pattern: state-funded museums under whichever ruling house happened to control the territory (the Grand Duchy of Tuscany's La Specola in Florence, 1775; the Kingdom of Naples's mineralogical museum), and — tellingly — after unification, the major new 19th-century museum in Genoa (1867) was built not by the new Italian state but through the private philanthropy of the naturalist-politician Giacomo Doria, who personally funded and ran it for over forty years, drawing on Italy's own modest colonial-era explorers in New Guinea, Ethiopia, and Borneo. This is consistent with — though not directly proven by — the pattern seen elsewhere in this research: institution-building tends to track a state's fiscal and political capacity, and a newly unified Italy fighting to consolidate itself simply had less of that capacity to spare than Britain, France, or even Prussia.

Russia shows the clearest example of a cataloguing project run as a direct instrument of an autocratic state: the Great Northern Expedition (1733–1743), commissioned by Empress Anna and organised through the Russian Academy of Sciences, involved over 3,000 people and roughly 1.5 million rubles of state money to survey Siberia's Arctic coast and reach Alaska. Catherine the Great's later invitation to the German naturalist Peter Simon Pallas (from 1767) produced an attempted full flora-and-fauna catalogue of the entire Russian Empire, published as *Flora Rossica* and *Zoographica Rosso-Asiatica* — imperial science in the most literal sense, commissioned by and reporting to the throne.

The Dutch case is different again, and worth flagging because it is the most openly *commercial* of all these models: the naturalist Rumphius catalogued the flora of the Moluccas while employed by the Dutch East India Company (VOC) in the 1660s–1690s, and his manuscript was held back from publication for nearly forty years partly because the VOC didn't want the information reaching commercial rivals — cataloguing Creation and guarding trade secrets pulling in opposite directions inside the very same institution. Only in the 19th century, after the VOC's collapse, did the Dutch state build a formal national museum (Leiden, 1820) and a colonial scientific bureaucracy (the Natural History Committee in the Netherlands Indies, 1820–1850) along more familiar state-run lines.

And Sweden supplies something structurally different from all of the above: not an institution, but a *protocol*. Carl Linnaeus, working from a single university chair at Uppsala, didn't catalogue an empire — he gave every other country's catalogue, regardless of its politics or funding model, a shared language to describe what it found, and then dispatched his own students (the "Apostles of Linnaeus") to Arabia, China, Japan, and North America to feed specimens back to Uppsala. Every state-funded, church-funded, company-funded, or privately-funded project described above was, whether its sponsors knew it or not, speaking Linnaeus's grammar.

## A shared vulnerability: what the 20th century took back

It's worth closing on a point that cuts against any tidy national contrast: whatever the funding model, the collections themselves turned out to be just as vulnerable everywhere. The Museum für Naturkunde Berlin lost its east wing and its whale-skeleton hall to Allied bombing on 3 February 1945, and its bird collection was badly damaged, though roughly three-quarters of the total collection survived because of an evacuation begun in 1943. The Berlin Herbarium was hit on 1–2 March 1943, destroying over half a million herbarium sheets and leaving decades of uncertainty about which type specimens had actually survived. Natural history museums in Stuttgart, Dresden, and Hamburg all suffered comparable wartime losses. And Britain's own Natural History Museum in London was hit repeatedly during the Blitz — an incendiary bomb through the botany department's roof on 9 September 1940 burned "many specimens and books" in the General Herbarium, and a V-1 flying bomb shattered the west wing's windows in July 1944. The instrument of destruction was the same war, aimed by combatants on both sides, and it didn't distinguish between a state-salaried Prussian institution and a Crown-funded British one. Two centuries of patient, differently-funded cataloguing turned out to share exactly the same fragility once the 20th century's own catastrophes arrived.

## Claims flagged as unverified or approximate

- The characterisation of Italian science as comparatively "modest" relative to Britain, France, and the German states is this note's own synthesis from institutional founding dates and funding patterns, not a directly quoted judgement from a historian of Italian science. It should be treated as a plausible inference, not an established fact, pending a dedicated look at Italian-language historiography of the Risorgimento period.
- Likewise, the broader structural comparison across all these countries (private/subscription Britain vs. state-salaried France vs. fragmented-university Germany vs. centralised-dynastic Austria) is this note's own synthesis of individually verified institutional facts. No single comparative-history monograph making exactly this cross-national argument was located and read in full during this research pass; a work such as E.C. Spary's writing on the Jardin des Plantes, or a dedicated history of Anglo-French scientific institutions, would strengthen this claim considerably if consulted directly.
- Precise specimen and personnel totals for the Dumont d'Urville voyages, and the full roster of La Pérouse's scientific staff, could not be independently confirmed from an English-language Wikipedia article during this research pass (fetch attempts failed); the figures used elsewhere in French sources are treated as reliable secondary corroboration rather than primary confirmation.
- The scale of wartime loss at the Naturhistorisches Museum Hamburg is described in German-language sources as severe, but this note has not independently verified precise loss figures for that museum and flags it as plausible rather than fully confirmed.
- Darwin's exact formal status aboard the *Beagle* (self-funded gentleman companion vs. a status closer to a sanctioned naturalist) is a live, explicitly disputed question in the historiography, not settled here one way or the other.
