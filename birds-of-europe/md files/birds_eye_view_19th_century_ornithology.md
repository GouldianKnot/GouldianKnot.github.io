# 19th-Century Hand-Illustrated European Ornithology: A Birds-Eye Landscape

## 1. Timeline: Major Illustrated Ornithological Publications, c. 1797–1896

**Thomas Bewick, *A History of British Birds* (Vol. 1 *Land Birds* 1797; Vol. 2 *Water Birds* 1804), England.** Wood engraving. Not a "grand folio" but a cheap octavo — significant because Bewick pioneered fine wood-engraving technique (white-line work on end-grain boxwood), making natural history illustration affordable and setting the template for popular field-guide-style bird books, in contrast to the aristocratic hand-colored folios that followed.

**Alexander Wilson, *American Ornithology* (9 vols., 1808–1814), United States.** Copper-plate engraving, hand-colored. The founding work of American ornithological illustration; Wilson (a Scottish immigrant) died in 1813 with the last volume completed by George Ord. Plates by Alexander Lawson. Established the US as an independent center of bird illustration and directly provoked/inspired Audubon's rival project.

**Coenraad Jacob Temminck & Meiffren Laugier de Chartrouse, *Nouveau recueil de planches coloriées d'oiseaux* (1820–1839), France/Netherlands.** Hand-colored engravings/lithographs. A continuation of Buffon-style plate series covering birds worldwide, not just Europe; important as a taxonomic reference work used by Gould, Temminck being a leading systematist of the era (also author of the earlier text-only *Manuel d'ornithologie*, 1815).

**Prideaux John Selby, *Illustrations of British Ornithology* (1821–1834), England.** Hand-colored copper engravings, many plates issued life-size (following the model Audubon would also use). Selby worked with engraver William Lizars (who also began Audubon's plates before Audubon switched to Robert Havell). A serious rival British project to Bewick, aimed at a more scientific, larger-format treatment.

**John James Audubon, *The Birds of America* (1827–1838), England/US production.** Double-elephant folio (~39×26 in.), copper engraving with aquatint, hand-colored, engraved and printed in London by Robert Havell Jr. Famous for life-sized composition and dramatic naturalistic poses; American subjects but produced/printed in Britain, reflecting the era's dependence on European (mainly British) engraving and coloring infrastructure.

**Johann Andreas Naumann and (from the 1820s) his son Johann Friedrich Naumann, *Naturgeschichte der Vögel Deutschlands* (core work roughly 1795–1817, expanded/completed edition 1820–1844), Germany.** Hand-colored copper/steel engravings, later editions moving toward lithography. Widely regarded as the definitive German ornithological work of the century; hugely influential on later German and Central European field ornithology.

**William MacGillivray, *A History of British Birds* (5 vols., 1837–1852), Scotland.** Primarily descriptive/anatomical text with more modest illustration than the grand folios; significant for its rigorous, dissection-based approach to classification.

**William Yarrell, *A History of British Birds* (3 vols., 1843, with later editions), England.** Wood engravings (small, in-text vignettes) — a return to Bewick's cheaper tradition aimed at a broad readership; extremely popular, multiple 19th-century editions.

**John Gould (with Elizabeth Gould as principal illustrator/lithographer until her death in 1841), major works spanning 1830s–1880s, England.** Lithography (hand-colored) throughout — central to the shift from engraving to lithography as the dominant technique for large bird-plate books after c.1830. Key titles:
- *A Century of Birds from the Himalaya Mountains* (1830–32)
- *The Birds of Europe* (1832–1837, 5 vols., ~449 plates) — plates drawn on stone largely by Elizabeth Gould, with backgrounds/finishing by Edward Lear, lithographic printing by Charles Hullmandel.
- *The Birds of Australia* (1840–1848, 7 vols. plus supplement) — illustrated chiefly by Elizabeth Gould (until 1841) and, increasingly, Henry Constantine Richter.
- *A Monograph of the Trochilidae* (hummingbirds, 1849–1861) and other monographs, with Richter and, from the 1850s, William Matthew Hart contributing.
- *The Birds of Great Britain* (1862–1873, 5 vols., 367 plates), discussed separately below.
- Gould died in 1881, leaving *The Birds of New Guinea* (1875–1888) to be completed posthumously by R. Bowdler Sharpe, with plates chiefly by William Matthew Hart.

**Richard Bowdler Sharpe, *A Monograph of the Alcedinidae, or Family of Kingfishers* (1868–1871), England.** Lithography, plates by John Gerrard Keulemans. Exemplifies the mid-century "monograph of a family" format Gould had established, and Keulemans's rise as the era's leading bird-plate lithographer.

**Daniel Giraud Elliot, multiple monographs (e.g. Pittidae 1861–63, Phasianidae 1870–72, Bucerotidae 1877–82), England/US.** Lithography, plates by Joseph Wolf and Joseph Smit among others. Elliot, an American, worked largely through British lithographic studios — London remained the production hub regardless of the author's nationality.

**Henry Eeles Dresser, *A History of the Birds of Europe* (1871–1896, 8–9 vols., ~633 hand-colored lithographic plates), England.** Explicitly conceived as the successor/expansion of Gould's 1832–37 *Birds of Europe*, extending coverage to the whole Western Palearctic. Plates chiefly by John Gerrard Keulemans, with some by Joseph Wolf and Joseph Smit. Regarded as more scientifically detailed and geographically comprehensive than Gould's earlier work.

Other key figures: **Joseph Wolf** (German-born, active in Britain from 1848), regarded by contemporaries as the finest animal draughtsman of the age; **John Gerrard Keulemans** (Dutch-born, working in England from 1869), the single most prolific bird-plate lithographer of the second half of the century.

## 2. Darwin's *On the Origin of Species* (1859) and Illustrated Ornithology

The direct Gould–Darwin connection (Gould identifying the Galápagos finches as distinct species in 1837) predates *Origin* by over two decades and is covered elsewhere on this site. The broader post-1859 effects were gradual and mostly indirect rather than a sharp before/after break:

- **Framing shifted from "created types" to variation and geographic distribution.** Pre-1859 works (Gould's *Birds of Europe*, Selby, Temminck) generally presented species as fixed units. Post-1859 monographs — Sharpe (1868–71), Dresser (1871–96), Elliot — increasingly discuss geographic variation, subspecies/races, and comparative anatomy in ways that reflect evolutionary thinking about speciation, even when not explicitly citing Darwin. No source found states Gould himself substantially altered his own taxonomic commentary after 1859 — he remained more a describer/namer than a theorist.
- **Rise of the "monograph of a family" format with comparative plates.** The mid-to-late-century vogue (Sharpe on kingfishers, Elliot on pheasants/hornbills/pittas) for exhaustively illustrating every species in a family, often arranged to show variation side by side, aligns with the post-Darwinian interest in comparative morphology as evidence of common descent.
- **Ornithological societies and journals professionalized taxonomic debate.** The British Ornithologists' Union (founded 1858) and journal *Ibis* (from 1859) became forums for evolutionary and biogeographic debate, running alongside the plate-book publishing world.
- **Caution:** no source directly asserts that illustrated bird-book *artistic* style changed because of Darwin. The trend toward more detailed, naturalistic compositions across the century is better explained by advances in lithographic technique, artist specialization (Wolf, Keulemans), and rising subscriber expectations than by evolutionary theory itself.

## 3. ICZN Case 3044

Case 3044, "Proposed conservation by suppression of all prior usages of generic and specific names of birds (Aves) by John Gould and others conventionally accepted as published in the *Proceedings of the Zoological Society of London*," was submitted by R. Schodde and W.J. Bock (*Bulletin of Zoological Nomenclature* 54: 172–182, 1997). It concerned a bibliographic problem: the *Proceedings of the Zoological Society of London* (PZSL) — where Gould, John Edward Gray, Richard Owen and contemporaries first named large numbers of bird species in the 1830s–1860s — was issued in serial parts whose actual publication dates often did not match the nominal year-dates on the bound volumes. This created uncertainty over exact priority dates, threatening many well-established Gould/Gray/Owen names relative to obscure earlier names with better-documented dates.

The proposal asked the Commission to suppress all such prior/competing usages and lock in the conventionally-used names, rather than force a case-by-case bibliographic re-litigation across hundreds of names. It drew substantial comment (Olson 1998; Bruce & McAllan 1999, among others). The Commission's **Opinion 2026 (2003)** ultimately did **not approve** the blanket suppression, leaving individual priority questions to be resolved name by name rather than by one sweeping ruling.

## 4. Gould's *Birds of Great Britain* (1873) vs. *Birds of Europe* (1832–37)

The observation that the 1873 work looks "markedly more detailed" is well-supported by the change in production team and the 35-year gap:

- **Artists changed substantially.** *Birds of Europe* (1832–37) was drawn on stone chiefly by Elizabeth Gould, working from John's sketches, with lithographic printing by Charles Hullmandel and background/compositional assistance from a young Edward Lear. Elizabeth died in 1841. *The Birds of Great Britain* (issued in parts 1862–1873, 5 volumes, 367 plates) was instead illustrated primarily by **William Matthew Hart**, who joined Gould's studio in 1851 and by 1870 was "Gould's chief artist and lithographer," alongside **Henry Constantine Richter**, Gould's principal illustrator through the 1840s–50s.
- **Technical/artistic maturation over three decades.** By the 1860s–70s the Gould studio and British lithography generally had decades more experience with chromolithography and hand-colouring workflows than in the early 1830s, when lithography was still a relatively new medium for natural history illustration. Hart in particular was noted for tighter, more naturalistic plumage rendering and more elaborate backgrounds/poses than the flatter 1830s compositions.
- **Scientific/illustrative conventions had shifted.** By 1873, illustrated ornithology generally (Sharpe, Dresser, Elliot) had moved toward more naturalistic, ecologically-staged compositions with more attention to habitat and comparative plumage detail — a trend across the genre, not unique to Gould.
- **Uncertainty flag:** exact plate-by-plate attribution (Hart vs. Richter vs. others) wasn't fully resolved in the sources checked; a specialist bibliography (Sauer's *John Gould the Bird Man*) would be needed for that level of precision.
