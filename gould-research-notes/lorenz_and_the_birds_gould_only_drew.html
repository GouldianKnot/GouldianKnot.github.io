What the Plate Doesn’t Show: Lorenz and the Birds Gould Only Drew

← All Research Notes

John Gould never read Konrad Lorenz. He couldn’t have — Lorenz was born in Vienna in 1903, the year after Gould died. Any essay connecting the two has to be honest about what it is: not a citation Gould made, the way every other entry in this Library is, but a deliberate then-and-now pairing, built on two birds this gallery already has hanging on the wall that happen to be the same species Lorenz spent his career watching.

Gould’s Birds of Great Britain includes a Jackdaw and a Grey Lag Goose — each rendered as Gould’s plates always are: a single, static, beautifully observed individual, described by plumage, range, and habit, fixed at one moment in hand-colored lithography. Both species are also, independently, the two animals Konrad Lorenz is most famous for having actually lived alongside. He raised jackdaws in colonies at his own house, learning their complex social calls and pecking-order behavior well enough to more or less join the flock himself. And it was a clutch of greylag goslings, hatched under his direct care, that gave him his most famous discovery: the goslings imprinted on Lorenz within hours of hatching, following him everywhere as if he were their parent, simply because he was the first moving thing they saw. The phenomenon — imprinting — became one of the founding observations of ethology, the scientific study of animal behavior, and helped win Lorenz the 1973 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine, shared with Niko Tinbergen and Karl von Frisch.

Put Gould’s plate and Lorenz’s goslings side by side and the gap between them is really a gap in what 19th-century natural history was set up to notice at all. Gould’s method — skins, measurements, careful plumage description, a single representative pose — was extraordinarily good at answering “what is this bird, and how do you tell it from a similar one.” It had almost no way to answer “what does this bird do, moment to moment, and why.” A Grey Lag Goose on Gould’s plate is a fixed specimen. A Grey Lag Goose to Lorenz was a developing animal, forming an attachment in real time to whatever crossed its path first — a fact no amount of staring at a mounted skin could ever have revealed, because it isn’t a fact about anatomy at all. It took a scientist willing to hatch the eggs himself and let a gosling follow him around the garden to find it.

The Library’s honesty policy applies here too: Lorenz joined the Nazi party in 1938, and wartime writings of his drew on the regime’s ideas about heredity in ways he later called out and apologized for directly in his own Nobel lecture. Historians examining his actual research have found no evidence his experimental work on animal behavior was shaped by that ideology — but it’s part of the honest record of who he was, not a detail this piece should quietly leave out.

None of this makes Lorenz a substitute for anything in Gould’s own library, and this piece isn’t pretending otherwise. But it’s a genuine, concrete link rather than a floating bit of intellectual history: two specific birds, drawn once by a man working from a skin and a notebook, later understood in an entirely different dimension by a man who let them follow him around his own garden.
