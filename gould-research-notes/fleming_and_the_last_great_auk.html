Gould’s Most-Cited Authority Was a Minister Who Held the Last British Great Auk

← All Research Notes

Open Gould’s Birds of Great Britain at almost any plate and follow the small-type synonymy line down from the Latin name, and sooner or later — on 44 separate occasions across the five volumes, more than almost any other authority Gould leans on — you’ll run into an abbreviation: “Flem. Brit. Anim.” or “Flem. Hist. of Brit. Anim.” It’s John Fleming’s A History of British Animals (1828), and Gould reached for it constantly — for the Golden Eagle, the Kestrel, dozens of others — the way a working naturalist reaches for the one book on the shelf that’s always right.

Fleming himself was an odd fit for a modern idea of what a Victorian naturalist looked like. Born in Bathgate, Scotland, in 1785, he trained for the ministry, not the laboratory, studying divinity at Edinburgh and serving as a parish minister in Bressay, Flisk, and Clackmannan for years before natural history became his actual career. He was elected a Fellow of the Royal Society in 1814 while still a working clergyman, and only later moved into academic posts — the chair of natural philosophy at Aberdeen’s King’s College from 1834, then professor of natural history at Edinburgh’s New College from 1845. Minister and man of science were, for Fleming, the same job.

That combination put him squarely inside one of the 19th century’s real intellectual fault lines, decades before Darwin ever published. Geologists of Fleming’s generation were finding fossil evidence that didn’t sit easily next to a literal reading of Genesis, and in 1824 Fleming took that fight on directly, publishing a sharp critique of the eminent geologist William Buckland’s theory that a single catastrophic flood — a scientific version of Noah’s Deluge — explained the geological record. Fleming argued instead for gradual climate change as the better explanation for what the rocks actually showed, and went further still: proposing that species now extinct might simply have failed to survive a shift in conditions they could no longer tolerate, a naturalistic account of extinction that needed no single cataclysm at all. It’s the same territory — deep time, gradual change, extinction without divine intervention — that Darwin and Lyell would make famous a generation later, and historians have traced Fleming’s influence on Darwin’s own thinking about instinct and the geography of species.

In 1821, on a lighthouse-inspection cruise around Scotland, John Fleming was handed a bird by residents of the island of Scalpay — a living Great Auk, one of the last ever recorded in Britain. It escaped from the boat before he could keep it. The species would be extinct within a generation.

That moment belongs to this gallery too, even though it isn’t one of the 44 citations: Gould’s own Great Auk plate, drawn for Birds of Great Britain from an already-vanished bird, sits a few clicks away in this same set of volumes. Fleming didn’t just write the reference book Gould kept reaching for — he personally held, for a few minutes on a Scottish boat, one of the last individuals of a species Gould could only ever paint from skins and memory. Two very different kinds of encounter with the same vanishing bird, thirty-odd years apart, from two men whose work ended up bound into the same shelf of British natural history.

Fleming isn’t a footnote here. He’s one of the working authorities this whole Library was built to surface — proof that Gould’s citations weren’t decoration, but a real record of whose books were actually open on the desk while he wrote.
