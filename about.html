About This Project

Summer Study Notes (July–August 2026)

I should say at the outset that this is not my core area of expertise. I have been a passionate ornithologist ever since receiving the RSPB Book of Birds (which I devoured at age seven or eight) and Tony Soper’s The Bird Table Book back in the 1970s. Sadly, no one thought to tell me at the time that a flourishing academic field existed for it! Along with classical music, where I grew up it was hardly considered “cool” to demonstrate knowledge of such dark arts.

My core passion lies in the history of ideas, particularly within the History of Science and the History of Chemistry. My favored period is the 19th century. It acts as a bridge: recognisably written in the language of our own time, yet inheriting 18th-century (and earlier) modes of thought that feel quite foreign to a contemporary reader. For me, the Romantic period of the mid-19th century offers a lens to reinterpret these ideas as a reaction to Kantian Rationalism. It followed decades after Alexander von Humboldt’s explorations, which marked early observations on how settler activity impacted coastal South American ecosystems and caused habitat loss.

In 19th-century Oxford and Cambridge, Natural History was taught as an unexamined course, popular among Theology students on the premise of “reading the book to better know the Author.” The resulting flourish of literature—titles like The Natural History of Selborne and various Histories of Birds—attempted to address a gap in public understanding and reflect a newfound joy in the variety of Nature. Brilliant minds, particularly “Parson Naturalists” like Gilbert White, wrote for a rising audience of literate city dwellers. White worked firmly in the mode of documenting everything within his small parish of Selborne, a former monastic priory. Curiously, his account remains in print to this day. I was eager to see how well his broad natural history connected with more targeted ornithological accounts by popular authors like Morris, Yarrell, and others writing toward the end of the century.

Having acquired some original 19th-century volumes, I began working with the AI engine Claude to explore the artwork of botanical and zoological illustrators. When I encountered John Gould’s The Birds of Europe, the project quickly became an obsession.

The bulk of the work—and the digitisation of Gould’s volumes—took place over an intense two-week period while I was enduring a severe cold. Unable to breathe or sleep, I worked around the clock alongside Claude, building the project layer upon layer. It was difficult deciding whether to delve deeper into the lives, motivations, and occasional obsessions of the people involved, or to maintain a tight focus on the comprehensive collection of birds.

Being me, I tried to do both at once. This is the result.

It became a strange ritual: trying to sleep at 10 p.m., waking up unable to breathe at 1 a.m., and resuming work straight through the night, day, and following evening. In this feverish, sleepless cycle, the project took full shape.

I realise, to my embarrassment, that a substantial amount of rewriting remains. That will likely become my Summer Study for 2027. This is very much a working draft, and will probably remain a work in progress year by year.

Reflecting on how Claude “thinks” has been a fascinating metacognitive exercise in how information is organized within a conversational dialogue—and how easily ideas get “parked and forgotten” if you aren’t vigilant! Conversations with too many branching layers quickly become lugubrious and unnatural. At its best, working with the AI felt like having an enthusiastic research supervisor—bringing to mind Prof. Duncan Bruce, Dr. Diana Manuel, and Dr. Peter Evans (AWRE), to whom I am forever profoundly grateful for the support, encouragement, and research approaches they modeled. At the same time, it acted as a willing team of research assistants handling the repetitive heavy lifting. I was merely conducting an orchestra—though at times I felt like Dukas’s Sorcerer’s Apprentice. Near the end (last night and this morning), Claude was consuming over 60 GB of my Mac’s memory, bringing the machine almost to a halt. Another learning experience! Perhaps I will understand my students better for it…

Naturally, the AI let me down with a bump. During a recent crash, it lost the database and had to rebuild, making me feel as though I’d walked backward a week. Rebuilding the citations database has been… a minor annoyance, to put it mildly. Yet another learning experience.

There remains so much left to explore. I hope these rough research notes and databases provide some amusement, and perhaps utility, to others. I use them constantly myself. Cross-referencing each bird with entries in Morris, Yarrell, and White’s Selborne has brought me back to the reading of my student days, rekindling that exact same pleasure.

My grammar school, KEGS, sat across the road from an old antiquarian bookshop where I spent much of my time after school. Many of its volumes dated from the 19th century. Reading them carries a quiet familiarity—a reminder of growing up in the same town (and virtually the same building, Britvic House) where another obsessive, J. A. Baker, wrote The Peregrine. It was a wonderful, eccentric bookshop where 19th-century poetry brushed shoulders with texts on nuclear engineering (which I bought and read) and full multi-volume sets of Thorpe’s Dictionary of Applied Chemistry (which I also bought, later gifting it to a colleague at AWRE).

That shop was a crossroads of ideas. I like to think of the human mind in the same way—an endless intersection of fascinations. Or, as Feynman so neatly put it, “just lovely conversations.”

JJ
