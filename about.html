About This Project

Just some short notes — on how The GouldianKnot came to be, and what it is (and isn’t).

This was my Summer Study in July and early August 2026.

I must say first that this isn’t my area of expertise. Since being given the RSPB book of birds, which at age 7 or 8 I devoured, and Tony Soper’s The Bird Table Book back in the 1970s, I’ve been a passionate ornithologist. Sadly, no one thought to tell me there was such a flourishing academic area of study in this field! Along with classical music, where I came from it wasn’t cool to demonstrate such knowledge of the “dark arts.”

My underlying passion is the history of ideas, unfolding in the History of Science and, in particular, the History of Chemistry. My particular period, for many reasons, is the 19th century. It is a “bridge,” recognisably imbued in the language of our own time, that also inherits 18th-century and earlier ways of thinking that feel quite foreign to a contemporary reader. For me, the Romantic period of the mid-19th century is a lens, a way of re-interpreting ideas, a reaction to Kant’s Empiricism that at the same time has a holistic appeal, with conspicuous antecedents such as Alexander von Humboldt’s “invention of nature,” as his biographer Andrea Wulf puts it. The natural theology of its time was a call to a selection of brilliant minds, the Parson Naturalists such as Gilbert White, to write for a new audience of literate city dwellers who longed for a countryside haven, a romanticised notion of country living. Some publications have held these titles, and a similar purpose, in England to this day.

I started working with the AI engine Claude to see how it could help me explore the artwork of botanical and zoological illustrators of the 19th century, having purchased some of the original books. I encountered Gould’s volumes The Birds of Europe, and this quickly became a passion project.

The bulk of the work, and the digitisation of these sets of Gould’s work, took place over a very intense two-week period, while I was enduring a particularly bad cold! As I couldn’t sleep, being unable to breathe, I just worked around the clock with Claude and added layer upon layer to this. It was hard making choices about whether to delve further into the history of the people involved, their different motivations and, sometimes, obsessions, or whether to focus on the comprehensive nature of the collection of birds.

Being me, I tried to do both at once. This is the result. It was a strange ritual of trying to sleep at 10pm, only to wake unable to sleep again at 1am, and so I would just resume work through the early hours and right through the day and evening. And so it was that, in this strange sleepless sleeping cycle, this project took full shape.

I realise, to my embarrassment, that there is a lot of re-writing still to do, and that will probably be my Summer Study for 2027. So this is really a draft version, and probably, year by year, it will remain a work in progress.

Thinking about how Claude “thinks” has been a metacognitive exercise in how information in a conversational dialogue is organised, and how, in conversation, information gets “parked and forgotten” if you’re not focused enough to notice! A conversation with too many levels becomes noticeably lugubrious and less organic for Claude. When the AI was working at its best, it was like having an enthusiastic research supervisor — thank you, Prof. Duncan Bruce, Dr. Diana Manuel, and Dr. Peter Evans (AWRE); each of you, your modelled support and encouragement, and your different approaches to research, leap to mind, and I am forever profoundly grateful to have inhabited that learning and research space with you — but also, of course, a very willing team of research assistants carrying out the hard graft of the repetitive chores. I was merely conducting an orchestra that at times felt like Dukas’ The Sorcerer’s Apprentice. Right at the end (last night and this morning), Claude was using 60+GB of my Mac’s memory and brought the machine almost to a halt. Another learning experience. Perhaps I will understand my students better for this…

The AI let me down with a bump. In one of its recent crashes it lost the database and had to rebuild, and I felt I had walked backwards by a week. So the citations database is having to be rebuilt. To say this was a minor annoyance… it was another learning experience.

There is so much left to explore. I hope these rough research notes and databases will be of amusement, and even use, to others. I use them myself, and find them immensely useful. Cross-referencing each bird with mentions in other works — the “Histories of British Birds” of Morris, Yarrell, et al. — and, of course, Gilbert White’s The Natural History of Selborne, has brought me back to reading from my student days, and a return of that same pleasure.

My grammar school, KEGS, was located across the road from an old antiquarian bookshop, and I spent much of my after-school life in the book sections. Many of the books seemed to date from the 19th century, and so reading these has a familiarity with a youth growing up in the same town where The Peregrine was written by another obsessive, J. A. Baker, and almost in the same building (Britvic House). It was a strange bookshop, where poetic works published in the 19th century brushed shoulders with a book on nuclear engineering (I bought it and read it) and whole volume sets of Thorpe’s Dictionary of Applied Chemistry (this too I bought, and later gifted to a colleague at AWRE). So this bookshop was a crossroads of ideas. I like to think of the human mind in the same way — endless intersections of fascination. Or perhaps, as Feynman put it, “just lovely conversations.”

JJ
